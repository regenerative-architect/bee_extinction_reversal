Bring Bees Back:
The 1,000‑Taxa Roster

Global bee science recognizes seven true families — but within them sit hundreds of genera, tribes, and named species, many quietly absent from places they once thrived. This atlas indexes that real diversity, lets you match habitat to the lineages most likely to return, and tracks what comes back to your own patch of ground. Built for policymakers, land managers, educators, and the students and citizen-scientists working alongside them.

Why isn't this "1,000 families"? Read the scoping note

There are only seven recognized bee families on Earth — Apidae, Megachilidae, Halictidae, Andrenidae, Colletidae, Melittidae, and Stenotritidae (a small number of modern treatments split Melittidae further into Melittidae, Dasypodaidae, and Meganomiidae, for a total of nine — this atlas uses the traditional seven-family framing most conservation literature cites, and notes the split lineages within Melittidae's tribes). That's settled entomology, not a design choice, and this platform will never claim otherwise.

"1,000" describes taxa — the genera, tribes, regional populations, and named species nested within those seven families — which is where the real diversity (and the real conservation stakes) live. World bee science recognizes roughly 550–600 genera and over 20,000 species; this build currently indexes 222 genera, 42 well-documented subgenera, 48 tribes/subfamilies, 78 regional guild variants, and 85 flagship named species — 482 real, individually verified taxa in total — with the architecture built to keep growing toward the full roster. See Attributions for exactly how this was compiled and its honest limits.

Science & Risk Logic

What "de-extinction via flower summoning" actually means here

This platform does not perform, claim, or imply literal de-extinction.

No technology — genetic, habitat-based, or otherwise — currently reverses true global species extinction in bees. When this atlas uses playful language like "summon" or "invite back," it means something specific and real: helping a species that still exists elsewhere, but has been locally extirpated or is regionally in steep decline, find suitable habitat in your area again. That is genuine, evidence-based conservation practice — habitat restoration, range reconnection, and population recovery — described with game-like framing to make it legible and motivating, not to overstate what's biologically possible.

Why the framing still matters for real outcomes

Most at-risk bee species are not globally extinct — they're regionally absent, range-contracted, or in documented decline (see flagship examples like the rusty-patched bumble bee or Franklin's bumble bee in the Roster). That's an important distinction for policy: it means the intervention that works is habitat — forage continuity, nesting substrate, and pesticide reduction — not a lab.

Data provenance & how to verify anything here independently

The taxonomic backbone (families, subfamilies, tribes, genera, and species names) was compiled from standard, widely-cited melittological literature and public checklists during this platform's construction — it is not a live feed from any database. For anything used in a regulatory filing, funding application, curriculum standard, or peer-reviewed context, cross-check names and status against a primary source: GBIF, Discover Life's bee genera pages, ITIS, and the IUCN Red List for conservation status specifically. Risk tiers shown in this atlas are descriptive summaries, not formal IUCN assessments.
